\textbf{Implication for evaluation practice.} \rev{Symbolic and scripted
simulation is necessary but not sufficient for validating an LLM/VLM
robot planner. It validates reasoning strategy and task decomposition.
By construction, though, it cannot exercise the geometry-grounding
failure class of \S\ref{sec:taxonomy}, since it never asks the model to
compute a quantity a physical sensor could contradict.} A
geometry-grounding audit, identifying every numeric quantity a prompt
asks the model to compute
itself and asking whether a sensor or paired action already determines
it more reliably, belongs alongside task-complexity benchmarking as a
standard step before hardware deployment.

\textbf{The cost of the LLM/VLM-vs-VLA choice.} \S\ref{sec:harness-rationale}
argued for code-grounding over an end-to-end VLA on data-efficiency
grounds. \S\ref{sec:taxonomy} is the other half of that trade: six bugs
is the price of asking a model to compute physical quantities it was
never trained to get right, and \S\ref{sec:grounding-layer} is what it
costs to pay that price down. We read this as a cost worth paying, not a
case against the architecture, since every bug in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}
was closeable once diagnosed, precisely because the pipeline stayed
decoupled enough to diagnose it.

\textbf{Case 6 remains the largest \rev{open point}.} Everything else in this
paper is either closed or diagnosed precisely enough to know what
closing it would take. Case 6 is neither: it is a trajectory-level,
multi-body contact problem, and \S\ref{sec:zone-spacing}'s
static-spacing mitigation does not touch its dynamic half. \rev{Closing it plausibly needs one of two things: a motion-planning-level
check of the approach trajectory against already-placed objects' known
footprints, or a settle-time verification step after each release so a
displaced neighbor is caught before the next pick. We have not
implemented or evaluated either. The first is naturally an
obstacle-avoiding approach skill on the harness of \S\ref{sec:harness},
though, treating already-placed objects the same way a mobile-base
planner already treats static obstacles. It is the concrete next
addition we have in mind.}

\textbf{Generalizability.} Our evidence comes from one manipulator
(UR5cb), one gripper, one camera rig, and a single underlying model
(\code{kimi-k2.6}) used for both the LLM and VLM arm. \rev{We do not
claim that the specific six bugs of Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} recur
verbatim on different hardware. We claim only that the \emph{structural
pattern} is a property of this architecture, not of this robot:
geometry computed by a model instead of measured, and multiple real
bodies sharing space over time.} Grasp width is the one quantity we still cannot cross-check
against depth in every case, since a single top-down sample determines
height unambiguously but not left-right extent; our best-effort
edge-inset sampling (\S\ref{sec:grounding-layer}) reduces this gap
without closing it.

\textbf{Reliability of operator-supplied labels.} Our semi-automatic
protocol (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) rests on a single operator's real-time
judgment, unverified by a second, blinded annotator. This is not a
hypothetical risk: an early pass over this cycle's data marked every
trial safe regardless of outcome, caught only by cross-checking the
safety label against the operator's own free-text note, and corrected
before \S\ref{sec:evaluation}'s numbers were computed.
